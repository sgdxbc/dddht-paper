\subsection{Decentralized Storage Networks in Blockchains}
\label{sec:bg:dsn}

\lijl{Before discussing DSN, should give some context about blockchain and the issue of state scalability.}
\gd{Added.}
It is costly to store data on blockchains.
For example, there are more than 6000 full nodes operating on Ethereum~\cite{ethereum-node}, and the data stored on Ethereum (which is around 1256 GB~\cite{ethereum-size} nowadays) are replicated to all of them.
The fully-replicated nature of BFT protocols causes large consumption on storage and network resources, and results in economically costly on-chain storage.
In 2021, the famous CryptoPunks NFT stores 10,000 24$\times$24 pixel avatars into Ethereum's on-chain storage.
Despite extremely compressed the data that still costed the developer about 12,205 US dollars.~\cite{on-chain-cryptopunks}
As today storing 1 GB data on Ethereum costs over 7 million US dollars~\cite{ethereum-cost}, which is unaffordable to most users and ridiculous compared to cloud storage.

Decentralized storage networks~\cite{storj2018storj, williams2019arweave, miller2014permacoin, EthStorage, filecoin, vorick2014sia, team2021swarm} are widely used to outsource bulk data from blockchains.
Study~\cite{balduf2022dude} found that for the top 1000 most popular NFTs, more than half of them are stored off-chain.
Another study~\cite{nft-apocalypse} reports that only 10\% of NFTs are stored on-chain, and about half of them are stored in DSNs.
As today, NFT.Storage~\cite{nft-storage}, a storage service based on the leading DSN Filecoin~\cite{filecoin}, hosts 704 TB data.
EthStorage~\cite{EthStorage} claims that it ``decreases Ethereum's storage costs by more than 1000 times''.
Sia~\cite{vorick2014sia} hosts report storage price from lower than 1 to 3 US dollars per TB.

\autoref{fig:arch} shows the overall architecture of integrating DSN as off-chain storage for blockchains.
Instead of the original content, a \emph{stub} for the content, \eg, CID with content-addressing~\cite{benet2014ipfs}, is stored on-chain, and the content is stored in DSN.
The stub is a tiny identifier that usually consists of less than 100 bytes, and it can refer to arbitrary large contents that is usually represented by a Merkle DAG~\cite{benet2014ipfs} that is rooted with the stub.
Blockchain transactions cannot directly access to the off-chain data during execution, but they can manipulate the data indirectly through the stub, \eg, transfer a NFT to another user or replace the content with a newer version.
In many blockchains, the storage nodes in DSN also prove that they have correctly stored the contents by submitting proof of storage~\cite{fisch2018scaling} to the blockchain.
The proof shows that the storage node has utilized its storage capacity to store a specific content at the given period, and the smart contract running on the blockchain can reward the node accordingly or penalize nodes for missing proof of storage.
There are active ongoing research on constructing proof of storage \eg with random sampling and Merkle tree-based inclusion proof.
The proof of storage guarantees that the probability for a faulty storage node to construct a valid proof without storing the data is negligible.

The major challenge of off-chain storage is to ensure durability.
As reported~\cite{nft-storage}, $\frac{1}{3}$ of all NFTs went dead in 2023, and the average lifespan of NFTs is 1.14 years.
Similar to permissionless BFTs, the DSNs must also tolerate Byzantine~\cite{bgp} nodes.

\lijl{People might be confused if we start the thread model with storage proofs. Maybe summarize the different faulty behaviors. Storage proofs address one of them, i.e., claiming objects are stored but actually not.}

The Byzantine storage nodes may diverge their behaviors from the specification in arbitrary ways.
They may pretend to store the contents or pretend to store more copies of the contents than actual, try to retrieve from other redundancy of the content upon requested, perform withdraw attack by storing the content but refusing to serve them for download requests, temper the stored content, etc.
The integrity of stored contents can be simply verified by recording the content hash and compare with it.
Proof of storage, on the other hand, allow us to continuously audit the redundancy.

Storage nodes submit proof of storage for the contents they stored in every \emph{proof period}.
The proofs attest:
\begin{enumerate*}
    \item the stored contents are durable with sufficient redundancy, which enables external auditing, and
    \item the utilized capacities of storage nodes.
\end{enumerate*}

We also assume that in a proof period the system is able to finish repairing for any repairable faults.

\paragraph{Alternative DSN approaches.}
We have different kinds of DSNs curated in \autoref{tab:dsn}.
We compare the DSNs in three metrics.
The first is whether they require explicitly maintaining metadata about contents \emph{placement group} i.e. where to find them.
The second is whether they are \emph{competitive} \ie whether they must compete for the same share of reward. 
The last one is whether they can efficiently achieve durable, that is, the data size that they are durably stored versus the total storage supply.
\lijl{What is a competitive environment?}
\gd{Explained.}

Some DSNs do not control the placement groups of contents.
Filecoin~\cite{filecoin} and Sia~\cite{vorick2014sia} provide a storage market and on-chain storage contracts and storage nodes bid for storage tasks.
Arweave~\cite{williams2019arweave} and EthStorage~\cite{EthStorage} do not use explicit contract and every storage node is freely to join the placement group of a content, and all storage nodes that in the placement group share the profit.
These DSNs are competitive.
A storage node's profit may decrease because the other nodes store more.
As the result, the large-scale storage nodes take advantage in such systems for their lower amortized storage cost and the ability of deployment local redundancy schemes to better reliability.
Indeed, there are only 1.6k active miners in Filecoin network~\cite{filecoin-node}, 1.1k for Arweave~\cite{arweave-stats} and 468 for Sia~\cite{siascan}.
This increases the centralization in the system, giving more power to individual nodes which may lead to faulty behaviors like content censorship.
It's also harder to argue for durability in a more centralized system. 
Moreover, in these DSNs the storage nodes frequently poll the blockchain or directly participant in the BFT protocols.
This is also an unfriendly requirement to lightweight storage nodes.

\lijl{We should make an argument that such approaches would eventually lead to weaker durability guarantees, based on our model and definition.}
\gd{Tried so.}

\paragraph{The DHT approach.}
Other DSNs directly control the placement groups of contents.
Storj~\cite{storj2018storj} centrally tracks the behavior of storage providers, and allocates the placement group based on the trust level of the storage providers.
Swarm~\cite{team2021swarm} replaces the centralized allocator with DHT~\cite{maymounkov2002kademlia} and allocates placement groups with consistent hashing.
The DHT randomly samples storage providers to form placement groups.
Since the sampling on storage providers is independent, the placement group contains at least certain number of honest storage providers with high probability.
Any faulty redundancy is detected by proof of storage and repaired timely.

In Kademlia DHT~\cite{maymounkov2002kademlia} every content and node have an \vid.
The \vid is usually the hash of the content or the node's public key to enable self-certifying contents and nodes.
Then we can calculate the \texttt{xor} distance between node and content \vid{}s.
A content is assigned to the $r$ nodes whose \vid{}s are the closest ones to the content's \vid, which becomes the content's placement group, and $r$ is the targeted redundancy level.
Kademlia provides an operation \ffind(\var{k}, \var{r}) to the nodes, which can discover the \var{r} closest nodes to \var{k} in the network.
The \ffind operation is implemented in an iterative approach that is expected to finish in $\log(N)$ steps where $N$ is the number of nodes.
To tolerate node failures and churns, the storage nodes periodically re-discover the placement group of the stored contents, and re-replicate the contents if the discovered nodes are different.
\lijl{Who initiates re-replication? If two \ffind return different results, would it lead to extra redundancy?}
\gd{Resolved. It leads to extra redundancy if \ffind result is unstable, but that is too subtle.}

Because the node and content's \vid{}s are uniformly distributed in the \vid{}'s domain space, the placement groups of contents can be considered as $r$ random samples of storage nodes that are uniformly distributed across all nodes without replacement.
So the probability of data loss in a time unit $\epsilon$ can be derived from the failure rate $f$, \ie, the probability for a storage node to fail in the time unit, and the redundancy level $r$
\[ \epsilon = f^r \]
If the failure rate is set to $f=\frac{1}{3}$, a redundancy level $r=13$ yields $1-\epsilon>99.9999\%$ \ie the durability is over ``six nines''.
The mean time to data lost (MTTDL) can further be derived as $\frac{1}{\epsilon}$ time units, which are about 4368 years when a time unit equals one day or 182 years when equals to an hour.

The DHT approach is the most ideal one for DSN with lightweight storage nodes.
Metadata management has always been the key blocker for DSNs.
Fully-replicated metadata on blockchains is not accessible to lightweight nodes.
IPFS~\cite{benet2014ipfs} decentralized the metadata \emph{storing} with DHT, but the management \ie the assignment of placement groups still happens on-chain (in Filecoin).
Only with the DHT approach, DSN can allocate placement groups for contents in a decentralized way.
On the other hand, the nodes do not and cannot decide what to store, so they do not compete with each other.
\lijl{Decentralized metadata is the key problem here. IPFS address how to store metadata in a decentralized network, but it doesn't solve how the assignment metadata is generated in a decentralized manner. The other approaches help, but their assignment strategies still have issues.}
\gd{Rephrased.}

\subsection{The Problem with DHT}
\label{sec:bg:problem}

DHT relives the burden of the storage nodes by taking the responsibility of placing contents on them, so it becomes critical that how well DHT can make proper placement decisions.
Since the network \emph{pushes} storing requests to the storage nodes, it's hard for the storage nodes to reject the requests or hand off to other nodes.
This is because DHT's consistent hashing is symmetrically applied to \fput and \fget{}: no matter whether the requested nodes store the contents or not, the following retrieval will be routed to them nonetheless.
The lack of stored metadata means that the outcomes of \fput cannot affect the later \fget.
\lijl{Why is this the case?}
\gd{Explained.}
However, as previously mentioned, DHT actually randomly samples the nodes in a uniform manner.
This means that similar amount of storing requests are pushed to every node.

The durability guarantees of traditional DHTs assume that the storage nodes always accept store requests.
The assumption is valid for metadata service like IPFS, where the actual content is stored external to the DHT, and only MB-scale data is stored on each node.
However, it's not the case for DSNs like Swarm which directly utilize DHT as content storage, and the durability guarantee may be broken because of this.
The storage capacity on the nodes may be exhausted.
We measure how far the storage nodes are from exhausted with \emph{available capacity}, the number of contents a node can hold with its unused storage capacity.
\footnote{In this work we only consider fixed-size contents and state that varied-size contents can be split into fixed-size chunks.}
A node with zero AC is considered \emph{overwhelmed}.
The overwhelmed nodes cannot serve any further storing request, and may cause serious durability problems if DHT keeps pushing storing requests to them.
\lijl{One way to rephrase this point: guarantees of traditional DHT assume that each node always accepts a store request. Violating that assumption will break the guarantees.}
\gd{Updated.}

We simulate Swarm to provide a comprehensive study on the available capacities of storage nodes in a DHT storage network.
The setup details of the simulation are stated in \autoref{sec:overview}.
As shown in \autoref{fig:ac-distr}, the AC of storage nodes in DHT scatters across the wide range.
For the nodes with the same size, their AC distributes across 10 different magnitudes (of the powers of two).
And the average AC of nodes of different sizes monotonically increases as the node sizes, ranging in 9 magnitudes even though the node sizes only spans across 7 magnitudes.
This is because the expected \emph{utilized} capacity of every node is the same, regardless of the storage node size.
And that same utilized capacity would bring the AC of smaller nodes more magnitudes down than the larger ones.
As the result, while some of the smallest ($2^{10}$) nodes have already hit zero AC and been overwhelmed, the largest $2^{17}$ nodes also utilized around $2^{10}$ which is less than 1\% of their capacities, and the overall utilization is just single digit percentages.

\paragraph{Problem with overwhelmed nodes.}
If DHT still pushes storing requests to an overwhelmed node, it can only choose to either reject the requests or evict previously-stored contents.
\autoref{fig:overwhelm} shows the consequences of both choices.
With rejection, the storage network hardly accepts any further requests when only about 10\% of overall capacity is utilized, and the rejection rate already increase to more than 90\%.
With eviction, the storage network starts to evict at only 6\% of overall utilization, and starts to lose data soon after that.

And even worse, the durability can be affected even when no new request is coming externally.
When the nearby nodes churn, internal re-replication may be pushed to overwhelmed nodes as well.
Not to mention in the case of network scaling down, which could due to the normal fluctuation or simply false positive caused by transient network partitioning, the redundancy kept by the left nodes ``collapses'' on the remaining nodes.
In such case, the overwhelmed nodes have no choice but to evict (either previously-stored or re-replicated contents), which leads to irreversible data loss.

\paragraph{The virtual node approach is not effective.}
The previous works~\cite{zhu2005efficient, zhang2010novel} widely use the virtual node approach to load balance in DHT.
In this approach, a storage node spawns variable number of virtual nodes, which participant into DHT normally.
In this case each virtual node receives similar workload, and the storage nodes that spawn more virtual nodes can receive more workload and utilize more capacity.

Unfortunately, the virtual node approach is not as promising as it looks like.
Our simulation (\autoref{fig:ac-distr}) shows that the AC distribution of nodes of the same size still varies a lot.
Although the virtual nodes that belong to the same storage node can share their underlying capacities, the small storage nodes with few virtual nodes are still easily overwhelmed.
Furthermore, the AC of storage nodes are still not balanced even if the AC of virtual nodes is balanced, as the storage nodes that spawn more virtual nodes will have a higher aggregated AC.
And lastly, the virtual node approach introduce extra implication of correlations among the virtual nodes.
As DHT is not aware of the ownership of the virtual nodes, it may accidentally allocate redundancy of the same content to different virtual nodes of the same storage node.
This results in a weaker durability than expected.

In conclusion, while today's DHT can \emph{balance} well across homogeneous nodes, it fails to \emph{differentiate} well across heterogeneous nodes.
The heterogeneity nature of decentralized systems captures this inability, and results in the suffering durability of DSNs.
