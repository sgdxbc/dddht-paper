\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Distributed hash tables (DHT)~\cite{maymounkov2002kademlia,chord,rowstron2001pastry} and the consistent hashing~\cite{chash} at their core are widely adopted in storage systems to assign the \emph{placement groups} of the data, \ie, the storage nodes that can assigned to store the data.
While centrally-managed storage systems like Ceph~\cite{weil2006ceph}, Cassandra~\cite{lakshman2010cassandra} and Dynamo~\cite{decandia2007dynamo} directly employ consistent hashing to avoid the performance overhead and single point of failure brought by a metadata server, decentralized storage networks (DSN)~\cite{kubiatowicz2000oceanstore,ratnasamy2001scalable,benet2014ipfs,trautwein2022design,haeberlen2005glacier,filecoin} can only leverage consistent hashing by wrapping a DHT protocol around it.
DHT protocols enable the nodes in decentralized networks to locate the placement groups of the data by performing an iterative online query, releasing the nodes from the burden of fetching and maintaining the up-to-date list of the contacts of the current online nodes in highly-dynamical environments.
Thus, DHT has been the cornerstone of DSNs, and is pervasively employed for peer and placement group discovery.

However, \emph{heterogeneity}, the unique property of decentralized systems, brings a long-ignored issue to the DSNs.
Unlike centrally-managed storage systems that usually operate a set of homogeneous storage nodes, in DSNs, the capacities of individual storage nodes can range from 10s of GBs to TB level.
However, Kademlia~\cite{maymounkov2002kademlia}, the dominating DHT protocol used by Bitcoin~\cite{bitcoin}, Ethereum~\cite{ethereum}, BitTorrent and IPFS~\cite{benet2014ipfs}, does not respect this heterogeneity.
In Kademlia, each node receives identical storage load, regardless of its capacity and available space.
In the DSNs such as FileCoin~\cite{filecoin} and BitTorrent, the DHT is only utilized to store lightweight metadata, which incur trivial storage consumption on the tiniest nodes.
However, for the DSNs that directly issue data placement with DHT~\cite{kubiatowicz2000oceanstore,ratnasamy2001scalable,haeberlen2005glacier}, storage nodes with small capacity can run out of space even when the overall storage utilization of the network is relatively low.

Meanwhile, since DSNs are lack of central controller and the data placement solely relies on the passive DHT protocols, they cannot reactively respond to the situation where storage nodes have running out of space and cannot participate the data placement that DHT allocates to them.
If the placement group contains a storage node who have exhausted its space, DHT cannot remove it from the placement group unless it leaves the network, while the node has to stay in the network to serve the data it has already been storing.
Existing DSNs fail to propose any sophisticated solution for this situation.
As shown in our simulation in \autoref{sec:bg}, Glacier~\cite{haeberlen2005glacier} rejects the new store requests, which results in rejecting more than 90\% storing requests when the overall utilization is just 10\%.
On the other hand, Swarm~\cite{team2021swarm} evicts stored content by their economic values, and data loss happens at a merely 6\% utilization.

In this work, we focus on improving the \emph{effective overall utilization} in a heterogeneous storage setup.
That is, DSNs should make efficient use of heterogeneous storage resources across the nodes, and achieve desirable overall storage utilization without exhausting available space on any node and suffering from degraded responsiveness and/or availability.

While previous works have extensively studied relevant topics, none of them fully align with our problem settings: achieve desirable overall utilization in decentralized setup with heterogeneous storage nodes.
The virtual node approach has been considered for both rebalancing for the probabilistic nonuniform in identity distribution~\cite{chord} and for weighted processing load for heterogeneous nodes.
However, we point out that virtual nodes hide the actual failure correlation of the storage, potentially misleading DSNs on setting correct security parameters.
The other solutions, including weighted DHT~\cite{schindelhauer2005weighted}, DHT balancing schemes for nonuniform key access~\cite{karger2004simple,misra2004clash} and consistent hashing schemes for bounded load~\cite{mirrokni2018consistent,chen2021revisiting}, all comes with their own problems for applying in the problem settings.
We discuss them in details in \autoref{sec:bg:problem}.

To this end, we propose \dht\footnote{
    \dht represents Discoverability Differentiable DHT.
    As we deprecated the concept of discoverability in a later refinement of the work, a new explanation of the abbreviation is under investigation.
}, a novel DHT that introduces substantial improvement for utilizing heterogeneous storage resources based on the battle tested Kademlia protocol.
To the best of our knowledge, \dht is the first DHT design that can \emph{equalize} the available space across heterogeneous storage nodes.
By doing so, \dht can keep the storage consumption under budget on \emph{all} storage nodes while utilizing most of the overall storage resources, regardless how those resources are distributed across the nodes.
The design of \dht consists of two components: classified distance and employing the power of two random choices.
We intuitively describe the design rationale of \dht and show preliminary results in \autoref{sec:overview}.

Our preliminary evaluation shows that \dht is an effective design.
In the case where vanilla Kademlia DHT is losing 7\% of stored content or rejecting more than 90\% of storing requests because of the overwhelmed nodes, \dht completely eliminates the overwhelmed nodes and prevents both of the problems.
This is because the available spaces are significantly more balanced across the nodes in \dht compare to Kademlia.
In the stress simulation, \dht can store data up to close to 100\% of overall utilization before overwhelming nodes and losing data, which is 60\% more than Kademlia.
In the future we plan to implement \dht in real and perform real world deployment evaluations.

DHTs are effective at balancing request load across nodes in a network.
They, however, make a critical assumption that every node always has sufficient storage capacity to store all mapped objects.
In a permissionless network where participants exhibit significant heterogeneity, such assumption is no longer valid.
Even when there is sufficient aggregated capacity in network, the available capacity (AC) on each node can have large variance due to different initial capacity, request distribution, and lifetime.
We validate this hypothesis using simulations in \autoref{sec:bg:problem}.
Variance in AC directly leads to degraded data availability.
Since DHTs distribute storage requests uniformly across the network, a node with insufficient AC has to either reject a storage request or evict existing objects when other nodes still have spare capacity, resulting in lower redundancy level.
The issue is even more severe when the network scales down (e.g., nodes churning and network partitions).

To address these limitations, we propose Differentiable Discoverability DHT (\dht), a decentralized storage network that augments DHT by \emph{differentiating} workloads on storage nodes based on their available capacities.
Compared to traditional DHTs, \dht ensures balanced available capacities across all storage nodes, regardless of their total capacity, request patterns, or lifetime.
Such property enables strong durability guarantee: the data loss probability can be derived entirely from the redundancy level, the proportion of faulty nodes, and the skewness of storage capacities across the network.
Conversely, given the faulty proportion (similar to existing BFT protocols) and the capacity skewness, we can determine a minimum redundancy level such that the data loss probability is negligible.
\lijl{Is the point that prior DHTs can't derive this redundancy level, or their minimum redundancy level is higher than us?}
This makes \dht the first work that guarantees data availability when operating under permissionless, Byzantine storage nodes with bounded storage size.

Our key insight is to redefine the concept of node \emph{discoverability} in DHT.
While discoverability of a node has been a binary property (whether the node is included in any DHT \ffind), we extend this definition to an ordinal value to represent how \emph{frequent} the node appears in the results of \ffind.
%that the target \vid is uniformly distributed in the \vid's domain space.
In \dht, the discoverability of nodes is proportional to the nodes' AC, \ie, nodes with higher AC are discovered with higher frequency, and thus receive more storage requests.
On the other hand, nodes with low remaining capacity receive few storage requests, reducing the likelihood of forced eviction and data losses.

\dht augments Kademlia~\cite{maymounkov2002kademlia} by introducing two novel designs to differentiate the discoverability of storage nodes with different AC.
The first design applies \emph{macro} differentiation of storage nodes by defining \emph{node classes}: nodes are classified based on their total capacity.
\dht implements this classification by modifying the Kademlia's \texttt{xor} distance definition.
Node classes approximate the nodes' AC with their total capacity.
A weakness of this static approach is that the node class cannot be adjusted based on the current AC.
\lijl{After applying node class, what properties do the system have? What are the remaining ``errors''?}
To compensate such shortcoming, we apply \emph{the power of two choices} (2RC) for \emph{micro} differentiation.
Specifically, each \ffind operation locates two candidate placement groups, and chooses the group with the higher \emph{minimum} AC.
As such, 2RC performs accurate local differentiation but only among a few nodes from the two candidate placement groups.
Combining the two methods results in balanced AC without degrading DHT performance, leading to improved data availability guarantees.
